A Brief Note From Me to the Reader (and not a part of the liner notes

It was difficult to make a selection. I'm a re-entry student at UC Berkeley, and I've listened to a lot of music over my 36 years. This is a small representation of tracks I've loved and learned from, curated to better answer the course prompts. My musical world is genuinely eclectic - Selena via my Mexican tía, classic Bollywood from countless hours in Indian restaurants and grocers, fado in my grandmother's car, Brazilian protest songs in my father's living room, classical, heavy metal, hair metal, Linkin Park and Silversun Pickups in my teens (Evanescence was my first concert! and I saw Linkin Park, The Offspring, and Muse at Rock in Rio Lisbon 2008), old school hip-hop, europop, first gen K-Pop, disco, 80's ballads, and the vast landscape of electronic music I've been exploring ever since my first rave at EDC Las Vegas in 2014. Somewhere in there is also a deep love for film scores, the occasional jazz rabbit hole, and whatever my friends put on at 3am after the rave that I'll never be able to identify again. If any of this resonates or you want to argue about music, hit me up :)

Saudade

Saudade organizes this mixtape not as a theme but as a structural condition - a mode of existence. My relationship to Mozambique and Brazil is constitutively incomplete: a second-order cultural displacement, absorbed through my parents' own experiences rather than my own direct participation. I also have a contextually unusual tie to being "American" that I will return to later. The longing is real; the object of that longing is not, and cannot be, fully available to me. It has no simple English translation, which is part of why it fits.

Adelaide Reyes writes that identity is both who you think you are and what others ascribe to you, and that those two things can be in serious conflict. Ronald Takaki argues that American national identity has been constructed through exclusion - "American" has historically meant white European. My family is a strange edge case for both arguments simultaneously. My paternal side claims membership in the Daughters of the American Revolution. My maternal side is immigrants - my mother arrived at 26, already twice removed: born in colonial Mozambique to Portuguese parents, a retornado who never belonged in continental Portugal.

I don't believe anyone has a greater (or lesser) claim to American identity than anyone else. But for those who insist on trying to police the definition - You can't out-credential me. I have DAR membership through at least 3 different ancestors. I was born here, my family has been here for generations. I beat the test by every formal measure exclusionists apply. And yet - I also have to translate at every medical appointment for my avós, fill out their paperwork, navigate every bureaucratic system on their behalf - the invisible labor of the American born child of immigrants that doesn't visually register on me, yet I know it well. If my quintessential exclusionary credentials don't make me American enough for some, and the immigrant experience doesn't make me immigrant enough for others, then the test was never really about any of those things, was it. Takaki might recognize the gap between formal national identity and its actual cultural contents - my existence sits precisely in that gap.

Alegria, Alegria — Caetano Veloso

Kay Shelemay's concept of elaborative encoding helps explain the internal feeling of connection this song carries - music encoded alongside an entire sensory childhood world becomes inseparable from it. The feijão, the woven baskets, the cloth parrot, the carved panel, the piranha - these objects and this music were encoded together before I had frameworks to understand what any of them meant, or where they came from. In a way, it formed my own bubble of an eclectic family diaspora.

Turino says indexical signs happen through co-occurrence rather than resemblance. I didn't learn this culture from its source; I absorbed it from the memories of someone who lived it. The Tropicália movement's hybrid electric sound is itself an example of music as political semiotics - its cultural impurity a sonic protest. Alegria, Alegria, its anthem, which Latin American History scholar Victoria Broadus describes as "scattered and ambiguous." For a family whose cultural belonging has never been straightforward, an intentionally illegible aesthetic feels right.

O Gente Da Minha Terra — Mariza

Fado's origins in Brazilian African syncopated rhythm make it a human collaborative experience - inseparable from its colonial ties and African roots. It was absorbed by Lisbon's poorest quarters, becoming the song of the people, despised by the rich, then co-opted by Estado Novo as nationalist symbol, and finally reclaimed — most recently by Mariza: born in Mozambique, singing inherited sorrow from Lisbon's oldest districts where she grew up. The colonial entanglement doesn't resolve; in fado it accumulates, is made visible. She inherits the sorrow and then generates her own.

Portugal has a long and particular relationship to race - what artist Marco Mendonça describes as a normalized "virtual absence of racist prejudice" - producing its own specific complexities. Genuine love for Africa has historically coexisted alongside structural dehumanization in ways the American racial binary doesn't quite capture. Mariza singing "esta tristeza que trago, foi de vós que a recebi" describes inherited affect - sorrow received rather than generated, which is structurally different from grief, and places the listener inside of memory and emotion that did not at first belong to them.

Diamond Girl — Cirez D / Eric Prydz

My father played Kraftwerk for me as a child and has always been a devoted Pink Floyd listener. Eric Prydz, deeply influenced by both, felt less like discovery when I found him and more like sonic recognition. The semantic snowballing of Diamond Girl is an example of what makes a great electronic producer: Fox the Fox's 1984 vocal fragment (both the band leads are Indonesian-Dutch) carries its original nuanced emotional weight into a new layered context, and with it, my own return to electronic music discovering a childhood musical self that I didn't realize I had. Imagine my shock when I heard Children by Robert Miles mixed in as a classic trance track - my dad played me this song! I had found its people (and miraculously, also my husband).

Nietzsche's Dionysian collapse of the principium individuationis - the self dissolving into collective participatory ecstasy - describes rave culture's moment of synthesis more precisely for me than Turino's communitas. The gender ratio in electronic music is genuinely quite rough, and navigating belonging in a space that wasn't made for you is something I’m not a stranger to (lgbtq+ videogamers represent!) - it's a sort of smaller version of the larger condition this mixtape is trying to explore. Rommen’s concept of creating a new community of diasporic people describes rave culture beautifully: it generates a place of beloinging where all are welcome. There’s a reason it appeals strongly to second-generation Americans regardless of disaporic origin. Its origins in Black and LGBTQ+ communities of Chicago and Detroit is almost too perfect.

All three songs are, by accident, counter-cultural - political protest music, music of the dispossessed, music against authority. Takaki wrote that America needs a different mirror. A mixtape built from the outside of every category it touches might fit what he means.
